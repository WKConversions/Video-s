# Visual hierarchy, simplicity and readability

Motion design begins with graphic design. Animation cannot rescue weak hierarchy, weak typography,
poor spacing or an unclear concept.

## One strong idea per scene

Prefer one strong visual idea over many mediocre elements. Each scene has:
- **Primary element:** what the viewer looks at first; the carrier of the beat's meaning.
- **Secondary support:** what explains or proves it.
- **Optional detail:** texture, context, polish. Remove it if it competes.

The viewer should know where to look immediately. Don't confuse visual complexity with production
quality: high-end motion design usually uses fewer elements with better scale, spacing, timing and
animation. Constant motion does not mean more elements; a few elements that keep working are enough
(`motion/animation-grammar.md`).

## Building hierarchy

Create hierarchy with scale, weight, contrast, position, whitespace, depth, motion, lighting, focus
and timing. Don't make every element high-contrast or large; hierarchy requires difference, and a
frame where everything is medium-sized has none. For each beat ask: what should the viewer see
first, what second, what can stay peripheral, what can be removed? Use motion to move the focal
point over time.

## Reduction

Use the minimum number of elements that communicates the idea. Remove redundant labels, decorative
icons, unnecessary shadows, meaningless particles, repeated copy, and motion that changes neither
attention nor understanding.

## Contrast

Contrast comes from large/small, dense/empty, fast/slow, bright/dark, sharp/soft, still/moving,
near/far, loud/quiet, complex/simple. Good pacing uses contrast across time, not only within a frame.

## Readable where it plays

Website and social videos play small and often muted: a 16:9 film in a website section is usually
800–1,000 px wide, and about 400 px on a phone. The first two intake-form videos (WKConversions and
K.B, September 2026) set interface text at 16–22 px on a 1080p frame and showed every screen whole
from one distance: sharp full-screen, unreadable where they would play.

- **The line's subject is the largest thing on screen while it is spoken.** Show an interface whole
  only to establish it, then push in or crop to the part the line names: the number, the label, the
  button, the face.
- **Minimum sizes** on a frame whose short side is 1080 px (scale for other sizes): headlines 64 px or
  more, anything the viewer must read 36 px or more. Smaller text is texture, and nothing may depend
  on reading it. Karl's benchmark film shows interfaces at about twice their real size
  (`references/benchmarks.md`).
- **It works with the sound off.** Every beat's point is readable on screen through the visual itself,
  a label, a number or a short headline, at those sizes. This never means subtitling the voice-over.

The size checks at 900 px and 400 px are in `evaluation/quality-check.md`.
