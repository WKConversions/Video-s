# Continuity

The whole video must feel like one piece of motion design, not a set of slides. Plan continuity as a
table before building, and read it top to bottom as if it were the film.

## Track every scene

| Scene | Previous focal point | Current focal point | Next focal point | Motion direction | Camera direction | Camera depth | Density | Energy | Dominant scale | Shape language | Color balance | Entrance type | Exit | Next entrance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Rules

- **Hand the eye over.** The next scene's focal point appears where the eye already is, or the motion
  carries the eye there. A focal point that jumps across the frame without a motion to follow is a
  break.
- **Exits name their entrances.** Every exit states how it produces the next scene's entrance, using
  something already happening in the current scene (`motion/transitions.md`). Avoid arbitrary presets
  whenever a motivated transition is possible.
- **Direction.** Motion and camera direction carry across each change unless the break is deliberate
  (contrast, a new chapter). Across the film, direction still varies (the variety budget in
  `design/composition.md`), so the film doesn't slide one way forever.
- **Velocity.** Match perceived speed across a change (`motion/easing.md`).
- **Energy and density** follow the planned curve (`planning/storytelling.md`): alternate dense and
  empty, fast and held; no flat stretches.
- **Entrance types** follow the entrance rule in `motion/animation-grammar.md`; the table makes
  repeats visible.
- **Callbacks.** A shape, object or relationship can return later to tie the film together.
- Continuity lives in these hand-offs and in the shared visual system; it doesn't require one prop in
  every scene.

## Check

After the storyboard, read the table in order. Fix any scene whose entrance isn't motivated by the
previous exit, whose focal point jumps, or whose direction breaks without a reason.
